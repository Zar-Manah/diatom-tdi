\begin{abstract}
Biomonitoring of freshwater ecosystems under regulatory directives (such as the EU Water Framework Directive 2000/60/EC) relies heavily on benthic diatom assemblages to quantify eutrophication via standardized metrics like the Trophic Diatom Index (TDI). However, routine assessment faces a severe operational bottleneck: manual identification and counting of silica frustules via light microscopy is labor-intensive, operator-dependent, and constrained by a global shortage of expert taxonomists. While recent deep learning benchmarks have achieved promising classification on isolated, pre-segmented diatom cutouts, computer vision models have remained largely isolated from ecological monitoring frameworks. To our knowledge, no prior study has closed the loop from raw microscopy imagery to ecological status verification or empirically characterized how neural classification uncertainty propagates into biological index variance.

Here, we present an end-to-end, open-source \textit{in silico} pipeline that infers the Kelly \& Whitton (1995) Trophic Diatom Index from raw light microscopy images, and we test the hypothesis that single-cell classification error damps during ecological aggregation. Trained on the 101-taxon \textit{UDE Diatoms in the Wild 2024} benchmark ($N = 74{,}194$ cutouts), a fine-tuned EfficientNet-B3 reaches $77.90\%$ top-1 on the hash-locked test split, and a closed-form edit of the classifier head raises it to $78.16\%$ without gradient descent. That split leaks: $45.61\%$ of test cutouts are crops of a micrograph also present in train, and accuracy on genuinely unseen micrographs is $75.79\%$; the clean generalisation number requires retraining on the slide-grouped split released with this work. Using the published index ($25\cdot\sum a s v/\sum a v-25$), the whole test set yields $54.4$ (0--100) against $53.8$ predicted (raw $3.1746$ vs.\ $3.1533$), with group-level Pearson $r = 0.9665$, MAE $0.1255$ on the 1--5 scale, exact 5-tier band agreement in $245/294$ groups ($83.33\%$) and $290/294$ within one band ($98.64\%$). A complete 12\,MP frame is swept edge to edge (1,118 windows) in $15.5$\,s on Apple M4. Code, model weights, split definitions and evaluation reports are released to support reproduction.
\end{abstract}

\textbf{Keywords:} Diatoms, Trophic Diatom Index (TDI), Deep Learning, Water Quality Monitoring, Error Propagation, EfficientNet, Limnology, Water Framework Directive.

\section{Introduction}

Freshwater ecosystems represent some of the most biodiverse yet vulnerable habitats on Earth, currently experiencing unprecedented degradation from agricultural runoff, municipal wastewater discharge, and anthropogenic nutrient enrichment. To assess and remediate these impacts, international regulatory frameworks---most prominently the European Water Framework Directive (WFD, Directive 2000/60/EC)---mandate systematic biomonitoring of biological quality elements. Among these bioindicators, benthic diatoms (single-celled siliceous algae belonging to the class Bacillariophyceae) occupy a preeminent role. Because diatoms exhibit narrow ecological tolerances to phosphorus, nitrogen, organic matter, and pH, their assemblage composition reflects integrated ecological conditions over weeks to months with high sensitivity \cite{round1991diatoms, kelly1995trophic}.

To translate complex multi-species counts into actionable regulatory metrics, ecologists utilize standardized biotic indices. Among the most widely adopted is the \textbf{Trophic Diatom Index (TDI)}, formulated by Kelly and Whitton in 1995 \cite{kelly1995trophic} and later recalibrated within statutory tools such as the UK Environment Agency's DARLEQ suite \cite{kelly2008assessment}. The TDI quantifies the eutrophication status of a river stretch on a 0--100 scale, categorizing water bodies into five quality bands: \textit{Excellent}, \textit{Good}, \textit{Fair}, \textit{Poor}, and \textit{Bad}.

Despite its regulatory indispensability, diatom-based biomonitoring faces a critical operational bottleneck. Standard protocols dictate sampling epilithic biofilms, chemical digestion of organic cellular contents using hot hydrogen peroxide or nitric acid, mounting in high-refractive-index media (Naphrax), and manually identifying and enumerating 300 to 500 individual frustules per slide under $1000\times$ oil-immersion light microscopy \cite{kelly2008assessment}. This procedure is labor-intensive (2 to 4 hours per sample for a trained expert), costly, and subject to substantial inter-operator taxonomic inconsistency. More alarmingly, the global pipeline of professional diatom taxonomists is contracting due to academic retirement.

Over the past decade, computer vision and deep learning have been proposed to automate diatom identification \cite{pedraza2017automated, kloster2020deep, tan2022diatom}. Most notably, in 2024, researchers from the University of Duisburg-Essen released \textit{UDE DIATOMS in the Wild 2024} \cite{venkataramanan2024ude}, the largest publicly annotated freshwater diatom image dataset to date (83,570 light-microscopy images spanning 611 taxa across German river catchments).

However, an acute gap persists:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Disconnect Between Vision and Regulatory Indices:} Computer vision studies evaluate top-1 accuracy on isolated cutouts, whereas ecological tools (OMNIDIA, DARLEQ, \texttt{diathor}) operate exclusively on manually tabulated count vectors. No unified, automated pipeline directly converts microscopy images into regulatory TDI scores.
    \item \textbf{Uncharacterized Error Propagation:} Regulatory biologists frequently assume that a $\sim 20\%$ taxonomic error rate in convolutional neural networks (CNNs) will corrupt ecological status verdicts. The empirical propagation of single-cell CNN classification errors into assemblage-level biotic indices has remained largely unexamined.
    \item \textbf{Ecological Database Discrepancies:} The widely used CRAN R package \texttt{diathor} contains companion columns \texttt{tdi\_s} (sensitivity) and \texttt{tdi\_v} (indicator stenoecy). Due to ambiguous documentation, researchers often mistakenly treat \texttt{tdi\_v} as the sensitivity index, invalidating assessments.
\end{enumerate}

To address these challenges, we present an end-to-end Python pipeline that performs multi-cell detection, taxonomic classification, and abundance-weighted TDI calculation directly from raw micrographs and mobile photos, and we measure empirically how classification error propagates into the index across the 294 slide/treatment groups of the benchmark.

\section{Mathematical Formulation and Error Propagation Theory}

\subsection{The Trophic Diatom Index (TDI)}
The original Trophic Diatom Index \cite{kelly1995trophic} assesses river eutrophication based on the weighted mean sensitivity (WMS) of benthic diatom taxa:
\begin{equation}
\text{WMS} = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{K} A_i \cdot s_i \cdot v_i}{\sum_{i=1}^{K} A_i \cdot v_i}
\end{equation}
where $A_i$ is relative abundance, $s_i \in [1, 5]$ is ecological sensitivity, and $v_i \in [1, 3]$ is indicator stenoecy. With absolute counts $N_i$, the implemented index and its linear rescaling to 0--100 are
\begin{equation}
\text{TDI}_{\text{raw}} = \frac{\sum_i s_i v_i N_i}{\sum_i v_i N_i}, \qquad
\text{TDI}_{100} = 25\,\text{TDI}_{\text{raw}} - 25,
\end{equation}
with the sums running over benthic taxa only: planktonic genera are removed from numerator and denominator because their abundance tracks pelagic retention rather than benthic nutrient loading. The quality bands are Excellent $[0,35)$, Good $[35,50)$, Fair $[50,65)$, Poor $[65,85)$ and Bad $[85,100]$. No benthic taxon in this 101-class set carries $s = 1$, so the reachable floor of the index is 25.

\subsection{Analytical Derivation of Error Damping}
Let a sample contain $N$ benthic cells distributed across $K$ classes with true counts $\mathbf{N}^* = [N_1^*, \dots, N_K^*]^T$. The true index is
\begin{equation}
\text{TDI}^* = \frac{\sum_j s_j v_j N_j^*}{\sum_j v_j N_j^*}.
\end{equation}
For a classifier with confusion matrix $\mathbf{C} \in [0, 1]^{K \times K}$, where $C_{ij} = P(\hat{Y} = i \mid Y^* = j)$, the expected predicted count is $\mathbb{E}[\hat{N}_i] = \sum_j C_{ij} N_j^*$. Assuming the valued mass is approximately preserved, the expected index error is
\begin{equation}
\mathbb{E}[\Delta \text{TDI}] = \frac{1}{N_{\text{val}}^*} \sum_{j=1}^{K} N_j^* \left[ \sum_{i=1}^{K} C_{ij} s_i - s_j \right] = \sum_{j=1}^{K} p_j^* \delta_j,
\end{equation}
where $p_j^* = N_j^* / N_{\text{val}}^*$ and the trophic distortion coefficient is
\begin{equation}
\delta_j = \sum_{i \neq j} C_{ij} (s_i - s_j).
\end{equation}
Equation (5) yields three insights. \textbf{Intra-guild invariance:} morphologically similar diatoms confused by CNNs typically belong to the same genus and share near-identical sensitivity, so $(s_i - s_j) \approx 0$ and the misclassification contributes no net shift. \textbf{Mean-zero inter-guild cancellation:} if off-diagonal errors between trophic guilds are non-systematic, positive and negative deviations cancel across the assemblage. \textbf{Sample averaging:} by the Central Limit Theorem, $\mathrm{Var}(\widehat{\text{TDI}}) \propto \sigma_{\text{tax}}^2 / N$, so regulatory counts of $N \ge 300$ cells reduce single-cell variance by more than two orders of magnitude.

\section{Materials and Methods}

\subsection{Dataset and Benchmark Partition}
We utilized the \textit{UDE Diatoms in the Wild 2024} corpus \cite{venkataramanan2024ude}, selecting all 101 taxa with $\ge 100$ examples. We used the deterministic split locked by SHA-256 \texttt{b6a0cb94\ldots} (full digest in \texttt{state/split\_f1.json}): $59,444$ train, $7,375$ validation and $7,375$ test cutouts. The split is hash-locked but \textbf{not leakage-free}: because it was built by shuffling cutout filenames, $45.61\%$ of test cutouts are crops of a micrograph that also appears in train, and all 294 test slide tokens are present in train. We therefore also release \texttt{state/split\_grouped.json} (0 shared slides, 7,482 test cutouts) for retraining; the checkpoints evaluated here cannot be scored on it because they already saw those slides.

\subsection{Taxonomic and Ecological Harmonization}
Primary sensitivity values were curated from Kelly \& Whitton (1995) \cite{kelly1995trophic} (column \texttt{tdi\_s}) and supplemented by DARLEQ2 \cite{kelly2008assessment} (column \texttt{TDIo}). Cross-referencing against independent DARLEQ2 records across 675 taxa showed that \texttt{tdi\_s} agrees in $80.7\%$ of cases, whereas \texttt{tdi\_v} agrees in only $5.9\%$; \texttt{tdi\_v} is indicator stenoecy, not trophic sensitivity. We vendorized \texttt{tdi\_s}. Of the 101 classes, 79 carry a published sensitivity and 74 carry both sensitivity and indicator weight; 10 of the 22 unvalued classes belong to obligate planktonic genera (\textit{Cyclotella}, \textit{Stephanodiscus}, \textit{Aulacoseira}, \textit{Cyclostephanos}, \textit{Discostella}, \textit{Pantocsekiella}). The pipeline isolates planktonic taxa into a separate \texttt{planktonic\_fraction} and excludes them from the TDI denominator, and reports the share of counted cells that carry published values as \texttt{value\_mass} (78.3\% on the test set).

\subsection{Neural Network Architecture}
We implemented an EfficientNet-B3 backbone \cite{tan2019efficientnet} ($300 \times 300$ input, 10.94\,M parameters, AdamW, cosine schedule, label smoothing $\epsilon = 0.1$). Inference runs on Apple Silicon MPS, NVIDIA CUDA or multi-core CPU through PyTorch. The surgical checkpoint differs from the baseline only in \texttt{classifier.weight} and \texttt{classifier.bias}: both come from the same training run (epoch 28), so the edit flips 19 test cells and changes no feature extractor.

\subsection{Spatial Detection and Counting}
For whole micrographs and mobile captures:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Calibrated sliding window:} window size $W = 150$\,px, matching the empirical median major axis of 148\,px in UDE cutouts, with a 0.50 stride fraction.
    \item \textbf{Budget-aware uniform lattice:} when the dense grid exceeds 1,200 positions the stride is widened until the whole frame is covered by at most 1,200 windows, sampled uniformly; the sweep never truncates to the top-left corner and reports \texttt{covers\_full\_frame}.
    \item \textbf{Three-stage de-duplication:} class-wise NMS (IoU 0.30) removes overlapping boxes of the same class; a fragment merge joins contiguous pieces of one cell that the window cut apart (same class, gap $\le 0.18W$, perpendicular overlap $\ge 0.45$, union span $\le 4W$); cross-class NMS (IoU 0.70) removes the remaining duplicates produced when the model changes its mind between neighbouring windows.
    \item \textbf{Optional refinement:} re-classifying tight re-crops with a $+6\%$ margin is available but disabled by default, because it dropped valid cells whose confidence was already near the threshold and deformed elongated boxes.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Empirical Results}

\subsection{Classification Benchmark}
On the 101-class test partition ($7,375$ images), the fine-tuned EfficientNet-B3 reaches \textbf{77.90\%} top-1. The closed-form head edit raises top-1 to \textbf{78.16\%} ($+0.26$\,pp, 19 cells) with no gradient descent. Accuracy is 80.98\% on the 3,364 cutouts whose source micrograph also appears in train and 75.79\% on the 4,011 cutouts from unseen micrographs, a 5.18-point gap; the headline figure is therefore an upper bound.

\subsection{Error Propagation Across 294 Slide Groups}
Grouping the 7,375 test cutouts by the slide/treatment token in the UDE filenames yields \textbf{294 groups} (1 to 133 cutouts, median 23). These are preparation units (\texttt{NH4Cl}-treated, distilled-water and \texttt{Naphrax} mounts), not 294 independent river reaches; they are the finest grouping the public filenames support. Table~\ref{tab:metrics} reports the group-level comparison of the baseline and the edited head with the published index.

\subsection{End-to-End Counting Accuracy}
Classification and index propagation are measured on pre-segmented cutouts. The full image-to-count path was audited separately on 60 real UDE cutouts pasted into blank fields, on three synthetic slides of 30 cells, and on a 902\,$\times$\,948 micrograph. On the 60 single-cell fields the engine returns exactly one detection in 49 cases, none in 5 and more than one in 6 (mean 1.05), and the correct taxon is present in 48 of 60. The three 30-cell slides yield 29, 33 and 32 detections. A cell whose apparent size exceeds the 150\,px window is sometimes split into fragments that the merge step does not rejoin, and low-contrast smartphone eyepiece photographs can return no detection at all. Counting is therefore the weakest link of the pipeline and the main target for an instance-segmentation replacement; the index results above do not depend on it because they consume human-segmented cutouts.

\subsection{Hardware Efficiency}
On Apple Silicon M4 (PyTorch MPS, batch 64), a single $300 \times 300$ cutout takes 15.2\,ms, a batch of 64 takes 142.0\,ms, a $902 \times 948$ micrograph takes 2.5\,s (121 dense windows), and a $5000 \times 3000$ field frame takes \textbf{15.5\,s} over 1,118 windows at a 114\,px step. When the dense grid exceeds the 1,200-window budget the stride is widened to cover the frame edge to edge rather than truncating the sweep, and coverage is reported explicitly (\texttt{sweep.covers\_full\_frame}).

\section{Discussion and Limitations}

The results support a qualified version of the error-damping hypothesis. Intra-genus confusions carry little trophic penalty, and inter-guild errors are close to symmetric (99 up, 107 down, 88 unchanged), so the group-level index correlation is high ($r = 0.9665$) despite a 21.8\% single-cell error rate. The effect is real but modest, and it is measured on a split that leaks: 45.61\% of test cutouts share their micrograph with train, and the clean generalisation number is 75.79\%. The index-level results should therefore be read as an upper bound until the models are retrained on the slide-grouped split.

\subsection{Methodological Limitations}
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Leaky benchmark.} The hash-locked split is not slide-disjoint. The reported index metrics are an upper bound; retraining on \texttt{split\_grouped.json} is required for a clean number.
    \item \textbf{Counting.} The sliding-window heuristic with fragment merging is sensitive to cell size and overlap. Cells larger than the window and low-contrast mobile photos are the documented failure modes; native instance segmentation (e.g.\ YOLOv8-seg or Mask R-CNN) is the logical replacement.
    \item \textbf{Optical scale.} $W = 150$\,px is calibrated to the UDE scans (median major axis 148\,px). A $40\times$ objective without oil immersion yields cells near 60\,px, so \texttt{--window} or \texttt{--scale} must be adjusted to the microscope.
    \item \textbf{Taxonomic coverage.} The model covers 101 Central European taxa and values 78.3\% of counted cells. Tropical, Arctic or hypersaline assemblages contain out-of-distribution specimens; the pipeline reports \texttt{value\_mass} and rejects low-confidence crops, but regional fine-tuning is required.
    \item \textbf{Single field.} The pipeline processes one micrograph at a time and computes relative abundances; it does not stitch a full cover slip or report absolute cell density.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Conclusion}
We have delivered an end-to-end, open-source pipeline that links deep computer vision to regulatory limnology, and we have measured both its index-level behaviour and its counting limits. On a leaky but hash-locked benchmark, a closed-form classifier-head edit reaches 78.16\% top-1 and the abundance-weighted index preserves the 5-tier band in 83.33\% of 294 slide groups, with $r = 0.9665$ and MAE 0.1255. These numbers are an upper bound; the slide-grouped split released here is the path to a clean evaluation, and instance segmentation is the path to reliable end-to-end counting.
